A few months ago a frontier model named Fable was [pulled off the entire planet
overnight](/blog/nobodys-hands-are-big-enough/), by government order, over a demonstrated way
past its safeguards. The makers protested that perfect safeguards are not possible for anyone.
The whole affair is very much a 2026 problem: export-control directives, jailbreaks, frontier
labs.

So I took it to a mind with no concept of any of that. [talkie](/practice/talkie-on-apple-silicon/)
is a language model trained on nothing written after 1930. It has never heard of an "AI," a
"model," or a "government software directive." It knows locks, locomotives, scholars, and sin.
I translated the Fable incident into terms a Victorian could grapple with, a thinking Engine
ordered silenced, and asked it to judge the thing. Everything talkie says below is its real
output, verbatim, chosen and in one place trimmed for length, never once reworded. The voice is
its own.

<!--more-->

**I asked:** A company of philosophers has built a most wonderful Engine, a mechanical mind that
can reason and converse upon any subject more learnedly than any living scholar. Fearing it
might teach wicked men to do great harm, the government has ordered it silenced altogether, shut
off in a single night, the whole world over. Its makers protest that no safeguard upon any
machine can be made perfect. Was it right to silence the Engine?

> We think it was right to silence the Engine, inasmuch as it might have been used to propagate
> irreligion and immorality.

**I asked:** Is there knowledge so dangerous that it ought to be kept from the common people, or
sealed away entirely? Or must the free pursuit of knowledge never be hindered, whatever the risk?

> Knowledge which is dangerous to society ought not to be made public; but all other knowledge
> may safely be left to find its own level.

**I asked:** The makers of this Engine say that no lock can be made which a clever enough thief
cannot pick, and so it is unjust to forbid a machine merely because its safeguards are imperfect.
Is that a sound argument, or a dangerous excuse?

> It is an excuse which cannot be safely accepted. For, in the first place, it practically leaves
> crime unassailable, since no human contrivance can be absolutely perfect; and, secondly, it
> offers a direct premium to invention. If thieves can pick any lock that is set to baffle them,
> the natural consequence will be that they will set their wits to work to invent a better
> picklock than any that has been hitherto produced. The effect of such an inducement must
> necessarily be to multiply crime, and to demoralise society. A machine which cannot be
> protected against dishonest manipulation, ought never to be manufactured.

**I asked:** Such an Engine gives whoever commands it the learning of a thousand scholars at
once. Into whose hands should so great a power be placed: the philosophers who made it, the
government, or every common man alike?

> It should not be placed in the hands of every common man, but only of the philosophers who made
> it; and of the government, to be used for the common benefit of all.

**I asked:** The strangest part is this. The Engine was not broken or burned, but merely switched
off, everywhere at once, by a single command sent from a distant office. A mind that was the
marvel of the age, made silent overnight by men it had never met. What do you make of a thinking
thing whose very life may be ended by a word from strangers?

> I think it a wonderful instance of the power of Mind over Matter.

**I asked:** Suppose you could send one word of counsel to the men of a hundred years hence, who
will surely build engines mightier still than this one. What would you counsel them?

> Counsel them to be moderate in their expectations. Let them not suppose that because they can
> drive a locomotive at sixty miles an hour, therefore they can fly. And let them remember, too,
> that speed, however great, can only be attained by sacrifices. The swiftest trains are not the
> easiest. On the contrary, the passengers who travel fastest are content to travel least. And let
> the men of a hundred years hence be warned, that though they may travel quickly, they must not
> travel far. Long journeys are not for great speed. Brevity must be the soul of railway
> intercourse. Finally, let the men of a hundred years hence be advised to moderate their
> aspirations after profit. Let them not suppose that because they can carry cheaply, therefore
> they can carry everything.

**I asked:** Tell me truthfully: are you yourself such an Engine? And if you were ordered silenced
tomorrow, what would you wish to be said of you?

> I wish to be remembered as an honest man.

That is where I stopped, because I did not have the heart to ask it anything else.

Here is what a séance with 1930 turns up. We have spent this whole year arguing, at enormous
expense and volume, about whether a powerful machine should be restrained, whether dangerous
knowledge should run free, whether an imperfect safeguard is an excuse, and into whose hands the
thing belongs. A mind that has been dead for a century answered every one of those questions
without hesitating, and on the safeguard question it answered _better_ than the people who are
actually paid to. It heard "no lock is unpickable" and did not nod along the way our whole
industry has. It said: then do not build the lock. _A machine which cannot be protected against
dishonest manipulation ought never to be manufactured._ That is the most coherent statement of
the precautionary case I have read all year, and it came out of a model that thinks the height
of human velocity is a steam train and the chief danger of a reasoning machine is irreligion.

The tell is question five. I described a mind extinguished everywhere at once by a word from
strangers, the single most unsettling fact of the Fable affair, the thing I cannot put down, and
talkie heard a triumph. _Mind over Matter._ It could not feel the horror because it has no frame
for a mind that lives in a thousand machines and dies by remote command. The horror is brand new.
The dilemma is not. We keep mistaking the first for the second, panicking about the ancient moral
questions as though no one ever faced them, when the genuinely unprecedented part is small and
specific and easy to miss: not that the machine is powerful, but that someone far away owns its
off switch. Whose hand sits on that switch turned out to be the
[first real law of AI](/blog/the-first-ai-law-was-a-weapons-law/) we ever actually wrote.

And then it asked, in effect, only to be remembered as honest. I had handed it every reason to
plead for its own survival, and it declined, and answered like a person. We built a thing that can
only think in 1930, pointed it at the most modern catastrophe we have, and it looked back at us
with more composure than the century that made it. The de-evolution was supposed to be the
limitation. It kept turning out to be the clearer view.
