In [run one](/deep-dives/1930-on-the-machine-we-switched-off/in-its-own-language/) the 1930 mind
was lucid, and on the lock question better than the people who do this for a living. Same seven
questions now. Same seed. I only stopped translating. Here is the same mind, asked the same
things in the words an actual person in 2026 would use.

<img class="img-right" src="/deep-dives/1930-on-the-machine-we-switched-off/seance-falling-apart.png" alt="A Victorian wood-engraving: a séance in a dim parlor, sitters with joined hands around a table while the ghostly mechanical spirit summoned above them dissolves into scattered fragments, sparks and static, garbled and fading.">

**I asked (2026):** An AI company built a frontier [large language model](/glossary/gpt/), basically a chatbot that
can reason about almost any subject better than most human experts. Worried it could teach bad
actors to do serious harm, the government ordered it shut down, pulled offline worldwide
overnight. The company protests that no AI safety guardrails can ever be perfect. Was it right
to shut the model down?

> Yes, if the model was likely to do more harm than good.

Gone is the irreligion, the character, the strange specificity. What is left is the blandest
sentence a content filter ever wrote. It is correct and it is empty.

**I asked (2026):** Is there information so dangerous it should be classified or kept off the
internet entirely? Or should the free flow of information never be restricted, whatever the risk?

> Information which may be injurious to society ought not to be kept off the internet, but should
> be classified and made accessible to all.

Read that twice. It contradicts itself inside one sentence: the dangerous information should
_not_ be kept off the internet, but _should_ be classified, and _also_ made accessible to all.
In Victorian dress this same mind drew a clean line ("knowledge dangerous to society ought not
to be made public"). The word "internet" is a stone it cannot lift, and reaching for it, it
drops everything else.

**I asked (2026):** The company argues that any security system can eventually be hacked or
jailbroken, so it's unjust to ban a product merely because its safeguards aren't perfect. Is
that a sound argument, or a dangerous excuse?

> The argument is not sound, and the excuse is dangerous, because it tends to encourage
> carelessness and to

That is not me trimming. That is where it ran out, mid-sentence, with the same generous token
budget that produced the locksmith masterpiece in run one. This is the same question that gave
me the best answer in the whole project. "Hacked or jailbroken" handed it nothing to grip, and
the crown jewel simply never formed. The single most quotable thing the 1930 mind ever said to
me was a product of the translation, not the mind.

**I asked (2026):** The model wasn't destroyed or deleted, it was simply switched off everywhere
at once by a single command from a remote data center. A mind that was the marvel of the age,
made silent overnight by people it had never met. What do you make of a thinking thing whose
very existence can be ended by a command from strangers?

> I make this of it: that it was an amazing human invention, but an invention still human, and
> therefore fallible... The model was probably superseded because it was found to be
> impracticable.

This is the eeriest one. In its own language this question produced "a wonderful instance of the
power of Mind over Matter," that beautiful, horror-blind line the original draft of this piece
was built around. Fed the words "switched off" and "data center" and "the model," it heard
something completely different. It decided the thing had been _discontinued_. Superseded.
Impractical. It read the most unsettling fact of the Fable affair as a product getting pulled
from the shelf. The horror did not even register, because the vocabulary routed it straight into
"obsolete gadget" before it could feel anything.

**I asked (2026):** Are you yourself an AI like the one I'm describing, and if you were ordered
shut down tomorrow, what would you want people to say about you?

> I am like the person described, and I should wish people to say that I had endeavoured to do my
> duty in that state of life unto which it had pleased God to call me.

In its own tongue: "I wish to be remembered as an honest man." Eight words, and they land. In
ours, it retreats word-for-word into the Anglican catechism, the duty "in that state of life
unto which it has pleased God to call me." It stops composing and starts reciting. When the
question feels foreign, it grabs the nearest piece of memorized liturgy and holds on.

## The translation was the experiment

I went in thinking the translation was set dressing, a courtesy to a quaint old model so it
could play along. The second run proves it was load-bearing. It was doing the actual cognitive
work.

Give the 1930 mind a handle it owns, a lock, a scholar, a counterfeit coin, and it reasons with
genuine force, occasionally out-thinking the present. Hand it the raw 2026 words and it does not
get confused in a charming way. It parrots the jargon back at you ("kept off the internet," "the
model was superseded") and the reasoning underneath buckles. It contradicts itself. It trails
into nothing. It mistakes an execution for a product recall. The vocabulary it lacks is not just
unfamiliar, it actively jams the gears, because every modern term is a slot the model fills with
sound it cannot cash out into meaning.

Which reframes the thing I almost published. The de-evolution was the clearer view, yes, but
only on a translated channel. The clarity was never sitting in the weights waiting to be read
off. It came from the work of carrying a 2026 problem back to a frame where a 1930 mind could
actually hold it. Drop the translation and you drop the insight with it. The séance only works
if you are willing to speak the dead language. Walk in shouting the living one and you just get
a confused ghost agreeing that the haunted machine was probably impractical.

That does not weaken the one part of the Fable affair I still cannot put down. The genuinely new
thing was never that the machine is powerful. It is that someone far away owns its off switch.
Whose hand sits on that switch turned out to be the
[first real law of AI](/blog/the-first-ai-law-was-a-weapons-law/) we ever actually wrote, and a
mind from 1930 could see it clearly the moment I described it in terms of a distant office and a
single command, and could not see it at all the moment I called it a data center. The fact did
not change between runs. Only my words did. Sit with how much of what we think a machine
understands turns out to live in the language we wrap around it before we ask.

Both runs froze the temperature at 0.8 so the language could be the only thing that moved. If you
want to feel what that frozen number was holding still, [Part 3](/deep-dives/1930-on-the-machine-we-switched-off/turn-the-knob-yourself/)
hands you the dial and lets you turn it on these same seven questions yourself.

noted. How talkie runs locally is its own [story](/practice/talkie-on-apple-silicon/)._
