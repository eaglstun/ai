A colophon is the little note at the back of a book that tells you how the thing was
made: the typeface, the press, the paper. This is that, for a website.

## Built with Hugo

The whole site is static, generated by [Hugo](https://gohugo.io). That means every page
is plain HTML, poured out ahead of time. Hugo does the heavy work once, before the page
reaches you. The theme is hand-written: plain CSS and a little vanilla JavaScript, about
seventeen kilobytes of it all together. Three small files filter the glossary, run the
search, and walk the art down the page on the one post that descends into a cosmetics aisle
at night. Two more scripts live inline on the only pages that need them, and both are
playgrounds that only pretend to compute: one swaps between 81 pre-rendered PULSE
reconstructions as you move its sliders, the other swaps pre-generated answers from a model
that thinks it's 1930 as you turn its temperature knob. Nothing runs a model in your
browser. Local development starts and ends with Hugo and those hand-written files. If you _View Source_, you'll mostly understand what
you're looking at. That's on purpose.

## Set in two typefaces

[Newsreader](https://fonts.google.com/specimen/Newsreader) does the talking - a warm,
bookish serif designed for reading on a screen. [Atkinson Hyperlegible
Mono](https://fonts.google.com/specimen/Atkinson+Hyperlegible+Mono) handles anything that
is code, or that wants to look like it: the model ids, the file paths, the occasional command
you're meant to type.

Both come from this server, not Google's. Google publishes every font in its library as
plain files on GitHub, so I took the two I use, trimmed them down to the letters and weights
this site actually needs, and set them on the shelf next to the pages. The italics are real
italics now, too. For a long time your browser was quietly taking the upright Newsreader and
tipping it over, like leaning a bookcase and calling it a staircase.

## Served off a Debian box

The built files live on a small Debian server I rent, handed out by
[nginx](https://nginx.org), which does exactly one job: give you a file when you ask for
one. That is the whole backend. Every request ends there.

## Search with no search engine

Type a word into [search](/search/) and the entire query stays in your browser. The server
never receives or logs what you asked.

Every page carries a 32-character string that describes it rather than names it: 192
yes-or-no answers a model gives about what the page is about, closer to a chord than a
title. Two pages about the same thing disagree on far fewer of them than two random pages
do, so _what else is like this_ is just counting, and your browser grabs thirty-four
kilobytes of the strings once, only if you visit search, and counts. Nobody wrote the 192
questions, either; a fixed random seed picks them. The first version asked 172 of them
straight off the model's own dimensions, and how that went is a post of its own: [172
Witnesses, Each One Half-Blind](/practice/172-witnesses/).

Your words never become bits, though: a plain match seeds the nearest page and the
arithmetic takes over. The word is the seed. The bits are the search. It's why "conscience"
hands you an essay about a chatbot that never once uses it.

Here's a way to feel how light a static site is. Everything you actually _read_ here:
every page of HTML, all the CSS, all the scripts, comes to about **1.5 MB**. It used to fit
on one 1.44 MB floppy disk, but it doesn't anymore. Sometime in the last month the words
outgrew it, and I took it about as well as you'd expect. So the site has moved up a format,
to the 2.88 MB extended-density floppy, the kind NeXT put in its workstations, which feels
right, since the web itself was invented on a NeXT. It fills just over half of one. Add every
image on the whole site, plus a few seconds of machine-made music, and it's about **110 MB**:
the entire thing, every word, picture, and sound, uses about one-sixth of a CD-ROM.

A caveat, because honesty is the whole point of this page: almost all of that 110 MB is
pictures, and most of _those_ bytes are me being stubborn. More than three-quarters of the
image weight is lossless PNG, kept crisp on purpose, out of a library that's mostly webp by
count and barely registers by size. Run the whole thing through 75% JPEG, the setting nobody
would ever actually notice, and every image on the site collapses to about **35 MB**, the
whole thing to about **37 MB**, roughly a third of where it stands now. It could go on a
diet tomorrow. I just like the pixels too much to make it.

So the simplest plausible computer that could serve this to the entire internet is a
**[Raspberry Pi Zero 2 W](https://www.raspberrypi.com/products/raspberry-pi-zero-2-w/)**,
fifteen dollars, about the size of a stick of gum, sipping somewhere near a single watt. It
would run Linux and nginx, hand these files to everyone who asked, and spend virtually
every second of the day idle until you go viral (please let me know how to).
If you wanted to be a lunatic about it, a five-dollar microcontroller with no operating
system at all could do the job too. The modern web feels heavy. This corner of it refuses to be.

## No cookies, no tracking

This is the part I actually care about. **The site sets zero cookies and runs zero
analytics.** Every byte comes from this box, typefaces included; your browser never has to
ask anyone else for anything. There is no analytics script, ad pixel, or behavior log. The web
server keeps the same plain access logs servers have kept since about 1995, and that is
where it ends. Nothing here is built to follow you around the internet, build a little
dossier, or work out who you are.

Here's the trade I'd make, and I mean it: I'd rather have one person email me to say they
finally understood something here - or to ask a question sharp enough that they'd clearly
thought about it before asking - than watch a real-time map fill up with a thousand
strangers worldwide I'll never hear a single word from.

One thoughtful reader beats a global heat-map of anonymous dots.

You came to read. You can just read.
